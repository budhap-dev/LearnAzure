## Why this matters

> [!SCENARIO] "If UK South disappeared tomorrow..."
> After the App Service incident, the board asks two plain questions: if UK South went down tomorrow, how long would the shop be closed, and how many orders would be lost?
>
> Dev's first answer is reassuring: storage is geo-redundant, the database has a failover group, and Front Door can send traffic to a standby in UK West. Then Priya asks him to walk it through. The UK West API reads its secrets from a Key Vault in UK South. Its Service Bus namespace is in UK South. Its Redis cache is in UK South. The private DNS zones only know UK South's endpoints. Nobody has ever failed over, so nobody knows how long it takes.
>
> The honest answer is "we don't know, and probably most of a day". Priya wants a real one, backed by a drill.

## Start from the business, not the diagram

[High availability](gl:high-availability) keeps a service running through the failures that happen every week: an instance dies, a host is patched, a zone loses power. [Disaster recovery](gl:disaster-recovery) restores it after the rare, large ones: a region down, data corrupted, an account compromised. Both are measured in the two numbers from [lesson 1.7](lesson:1.7): [RTO](gl:rto), how long you can be down, and [RPO](gl:rpo), how much data you can lose.

Set them **per business capability**, not per system, because they differ and so does the price of meeting them:

| Capability | RTO | RPO | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Checkout and orders | 30 minutes | Under a minute | Lost orders are lost money and lost trust |
| Browsing the catalogue | 15 minutes | A day | Mostly cached; stale prices for an hour are acceptable |
| Reporting and analytics | 24 hours | 24 hours | Nobody needs yesterday's dashboard during a regional disaster |

==Every nine and every minute of RTO costs more than the last; buy the level each capability actually needs, and write down the capabilities that do not need it.==

## Within a region: zones and resilient code

Most failures are small and local, and the cheapest protection is inside one region. **Zone-redundant** services - App Service, Azure SQL, Service Bus Premium, Redis, storage with ZRS - survive a whole data centre failing, usually for little or no extra cost ([lesson 1.3](lesson:1.3)). Start there.

The rest is in the code, because dependencies misbehave far more often than regions fail:

| Pattern | Protects against | In the shop |
|---|---|---|
| **[Retry with exponential backoff](gl:retry-with-exponential-backoff)** and jitter | [Transient faults](gl:transient-fault): a dropped connection, a failover, a 429 | Retry SQL and payment calls 3 times with growing, randomised delays - and only for idempotent operations |
| **[Circuit breaker](gl:circuit-breaker)** | A dependency that is down, where retries only make it worse | After 10 failures in 30 seconds, stop calling recommendations for a minute and show best-sellers instead |
| **[Bulkhead](gl:bulkhead)** | One slow dependency exhausting connections for everything | Reports use their own App Service plan and a read replica, so a heavy report cannot starve checkout |
| **Queue-based load levelling** | Spikes a downstream system cannot absorb | Orders queue on Service Bus ([lesson 5.1](lesson:5.1)); the warehouse drains at its own pace |
| **Graceful degradation** | Losing a non-essential feature | Reviews unavailable? Hide the widget; never fail the product page |

Retries without jitter and circuit breakers cause **retry storms**: every client retries at the same moment and knocks the recovering dependency straight back over.

## Across regions: four strategies

For regional disasters, the options differ in cost and speed:

| Strategy | What runs in the second region | Typical RTO | Relative cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Backup and restore** | Nothing; restore from geo-redundant backups ([lesson 3.6](lesson:3.6)) | Hours to a day | Lowest |
| **Pilot light** | Data replicated; compute defined in code but off or minimal | About an hour | Low |
| **Warm standby** | A scaled-down, running copy that Front Door can switch to | Minutes | Medium |
| **[Active-active](gl:active-active)** | Full capacity in both regions, both serving users | Near zero | Highest, plus data complexity |

The first three are forms of **[active-passive](gl:active-passive)**: one region serves and the other waits. Active-active sounds best and is hardest. Two regions accepting writes need data that can be written in both - Cosmos DB multi-region writes ([lesson 3.3](lesson:3.3)), or data partitioned by geography - and a plan for conflicts. For most transactional systems on Azure SQL, warm standby is the realistic top tier.

## The whole stack must fail over

A failover plan fails at its weakest component. Walk every one:

| Component | Shop's DR approach |
|---|---|
| Entry | Front Door with priority origins and health probes ([lesson 4.2](lesson:4.2)) |
| Compute | App Service in UK West, deployed by the same pipeline on every release |
| Relational data | Azure SQL [failover group](gl:failover-group): asynchronous replication, one listener name ([lesson 3.2](lesson:3.2)) |
| Files and images | RA-GZRS storage, readable in UK West |
| Messaging | Standard Service Bus has no geo-replication, so a namespace in UK West is created by code; the outbox re-publishes unsent events from the replicated database ([lesson 5.2](lesson:5.2)) |
| Secrets and config | A second Key Vault and App Configuration store in UK West, filled by the pipeline |
| Cache | Redis in UK West starts cold and warms itself |
| Private DNS | UK West private endpoints registered in the same private DNS zones |
| Identity | Entra ID is global; managed identities exist in both regions |

## Proving it

A DR plan nobody has run is a hypothesis. Three practices turn it into a fact.

- **DR drills.** Once a quarter, in business hours, fail checkout over to UK West on purpose, following the runbook. Measure the real RTO and RPO, note every step that did not work, then fail back. Failing back is also a process to rehearse.
- **[Chaos engineering](gl:chaos-engineering).** **Azure Chaos Studio** injects faults in controlled experiments: stop VMs, add network latency to a dependency, deny access to Key Vault, simulate a zone going down. Run experiments in staging first, then carefully in production with automatic stop conditions. The question is always: *did the system behave the way we said it would?*
- **[Load testing](gl:load-testing).** **Azure Load Testing** runs JMeter or Locust scripts at scale from Azure. Run the checkout journey at three times last Black Friday's peak, a month before the sale, and find the breaking point on a Tuesday afternoon rather than on the day.

## In practice

The shop's answer to the board, after the first drill: **checkout** fails over to a warm standby in UK West in 22 minutes, measured, with under a minute of orders at risk - and those are recovered by the outbox. **Browsing** keeps working from Front Door's cache and the UK West API. **Reporting** is restored from backup within a day, by design. The standby adds roughly a third to the checkout stack's monthly cost.

Monthly chaos experiments in staging and a load test before each sale keep the answer true.

**What breaks.**

- **Compute fails over, dependencies do not.** The UK West API still calling UK South's Key Vault is the most common DR failure. Walk every component.
- **The untested runbook.** A renamed resource, an expired credential, a step only one person knew.
- **Retry storms.** Clients retrying in lockstep keep a recovering service down. Add jitter and circuit breakers.
- **Capacity in the second region.** A standby scaled down to one instance cannot take full traffic straight away. Know how long scaling takes, and pre-scale for sales.
- **Nobody plans the failback.** The drill is only half done until primary is back and replication runs the right way again.

## Map it to what you know

| You already know | On Azure |
|---|---|
| A SQL Server availability group across two data centres | A failover group across UK South and UK West |
| A DR site with servers powered off | Pilot light, with the servers defined in code |
| Polly in .NET, or `opossum` in Node | Retry and circuit-breaker policies in the app |
| Netflix's Chaos Monkey | Azure Chaos Studio |
| JMeter, k6 or Locust on a spare machine | Azure Load Testing |
| The business continuity plan in a binder | RTO and RPO per capability, a runbook, and a drill log |

## Guiding your team

> [!TEAM]
> For every critical journey, ask:
>
> 1. **What are its RTO and RPO, and who in the business agreed them?**
> 2. **Walk every component: where does each one run after a regional failover?**
> 3. **When did we last fail over for real, and what did we measure?**
> 4. **Which dependency failures does the code tolerate - retries with jitter, circuit breakers, fallbacks?**
> 5. **What load have we proven, and how does that compare to the next peak?**

## Key takeaways

- Set RTO and RPO per business capability; pay for the level each needs and record the ones that need less.
- Use zones and zone-redundant services first. Then make the code resilient with retries plus backoff and jitter, circuit breakers, bulkheads, queues and graceful degradation.
- Cross-region strategies range from backup and restore through pilot light and warm standby to active-active; active-active needs data designed for writes in two regions.
- Every component must fail over: entry, compute, data, messaging, secrets, config, cache and DNS.
- Prove it with quarterly DR drills, chaos experiments and load tests at more than the expected peak.
