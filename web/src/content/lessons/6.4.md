## Why this matters

> [!SCENARIO] 140 alerts a week, and the one that mattered was missed
> Dev's inbox receives about 140 alert emails a week. *CPU above 70%* fires for each of the six App Service instances every busy lunchtime. *Dependency failure anomaly* arrives most nights. *DTU above 80%* arrives whenever reports run. Everyone has a mail rule that files them unread.
>
> Last month checkout failed for one in five shoppers for forty minutes, and 1,200 orders sat in a dead-letter queue. Both raised alerts, and both were filed with the rest. And when UK South had its App Service incident ([lesson 4.2](lesson:4.2)), Azure's notice went to the email of the contractor who created the subscription.
>
> Priya wants the opposite of what they have: very few alerts, each of which means a shopper is being hurt, and each of which reaches a person who knows what to do.

## What an alert is made of

An **[alert rule](gl:alert-rule)** watches a signal, tests a condition on a schedule, and raises an alert with a **severity** from Sev0 (critical) to Sev4 (verbose). When it fires, it calls one or more **[action groups](gl:action-group)**: reusable lists of who and what to notify - push, SMS, voice, email - and what to run: a webhook to PagerDuty or Opsgenie, a Logic App, a function, an automation runbook.

| Rule type | Watches | Speed | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Metric alert** | Platform or custom metrics | About a minute | Queue length, dead-letter count, request failures, dynamic thresholds on seasonal traffic |
| **Log search alert** | A KQL query, run every few minutes | Minutes | Error rates, SLO burn, anything that needs a join or a percentile |
| **Activity log alert** | Control-plane events and Service Health | Minutes | A resource deleted, a policy changed, an Azure incident in your region |

Alerts are **stateful**: they fire, and they resolve themselves when the condition clears, so the on-call engineer sees both ends. They are also cheap - cents to a few dollars per rule per month. What they cost is attention.

## Page on symptoms, not causes

The shop's 140 emails were almost all **causes**: CPU, DTU, a dependency blip. Causes matter for diagnosis, but a busy CPU is not a problem if shoppers are served. **Symptoms** - failed checkouts, slow pages, an unreachable site, orders not processed - are what shoppers feel.

==Page a person only when users are being hurt or soon will be; route everything else to a dashboard or a ticket for working hours.==

| Signal | Route |
|---|---|
| Checkout error rate or latency breaking its objective | **Page** the on-call engineer (Sev1) |
| Availability test failing from two or more locations | **Page** (Sev1) |
| Messages in a dead-letter queue, or the oldest message older than 10 minutes | **Notify** the team channel now; page out of hours if it grows (Sev2) |
| Azure Service Health incident for our services and regions | **Notify** on-call (Sev2) |
| CPU, memory, DTU trends | **Dashboard**, or a ticket if sustained for days (Sev4) |

## SLIs, SLOs and error budgets

To page on symptoms you need to define them. A **[service level indicator](gl:service-level-indicator)** (SLI) measures what users experience - *the percentage of checkout requests that succeed in under two seconds*. A **[service level objective](gl:service-level-objective)** (SLO) is the target for it - *99.5% over 30 days*. It is an internal goal, set tighter than any [SLA](gl:sla) you publish ([lesson 1.7](lesson:1.7)), so you break the SLO long before you owe anyone a service credit.

The gap between the SLO and 100% is the **[error budget](gl:error-budget)**. For 99.5% over 30 days, that is about 3.6 hours of bad checkouts a month. Two things follow:

- **Alert on how fast the budget is burning.** A *fast-burn* alert fires if the last hour's error rate would spend the month's budget in about two days - that is an outage, and it pages. A *slow-burn* alert catches a steady leak over six hours and goes to the team channel. Both are log search alerts over `AppRequests`.
- **Spend the budget deliberately.** While budget remains, the team ships features. When it runs out, reliability work comes first until it recovers. That turns "is the shop reliable enough?" from an argument into a number.

## Routing it: action groups, processing rules and Service Health

Priya's action groups:

- `ag-shop-sev1`: push notification and voice call to the on-call phone through the paging tool, plus a post to the incident channel.
- `ag-shop-sev2`: team channel, and a page if unacknowledged after 30 minutes out of hours.
- `ag-shop-sev3`: team channel and a ticket.

**[Alert processing rules](gl:alert-processing-rule)** act on alerts after they fire. They silence notifications during a planned maintenance window, so the release on Tuesday night does not page anyone, or add an action group to every alert in a subscription at once.

**[Azure Service Health](gl:azure-service-health)** reports Azure's own problems - service incidents, planned maintenance, health and security advisories - for the services and regions you use. **Resource Health** says whether one specific resource is healthy from Azure's side. Create Service Health alerts for the shop's services in UK South and UK West, routed to on-call. Never leave them going to whoever created the subscription.

## On-call that works

Tools do not make on-call humane; habits do.

- **A rotation**, primary and secondary, with an escalation if a page is not acknowledged in 15 minutes.
- **A [runbook](gl:runbook) link in every alert**: what this alert means, the first three things to check (the *Shop health* workbook from [lesson 6.2](lesson:6.2) is step one), how to mitigate, and who to call. Some steps can be automated.
- **Mitigate first, diagnose later.** Swap back the slot, fail over, scale out; find the root cause when shoppers are served again.
- **A weekly alert review.** Every alert that fired: was it actionable? If not, tune it or delete it. Pages per shift is a metric the team owns.
- **Blameless post-incident reviews** with actions that have owners and dates, including "which alert should have caught this sooner?"

## In practice

The shop's alert set is short enough to read in a minute: checkout SLO fast burn (Sev1) and slow burn (Sev2); availability tests failing from two or more locations (Sev1); any dead-lettered message and any message older than 10 minutes (Sev2); Service Health for the shop's services (Sev2); a spike in WAF blocks (Sev3); the workspace and subscription budgets to finance ([cost alerts](gl:cost-alert)). CPU and DTU alerts are deleted and live on the workbook instead.

Pages drop from an unreadable 140 emails a week to one or two real pages a month, and the next dead-letter build-up is seen within ten minutes.

**What breaks.**

- **Alert fatigue.** Too many alerts means none are read. Every alert must be actionable, or it goes.
- **Alerts to a person's email.** People go on holiday and leave. Route to action groups that point at rotations.
- **One alert per instance.** Six instances, six emails, one problem. Alert on the aggregate, or split by a meaningful dimension.
- **Thresholds set once.** Traffic changes. Review them, or use dynamic thresholds for seasonal metrics.
- **Maintenance silenced forever.** A processing rule without an end date hides the next real outage.

## Map it to what you know

| You already know | In Azure Monitor |
|---|---|
| Nagios, Zabbix or SCOM checks | Metric, log search and activity log alert rules |
| SCOM maintenance mode | Alert processing rules |
| An ops distribution list | Action groups pointing at a rotation and a paging tool |
| PagerDuty or Opsgenie | Still used, called from the action group by webhook |
| A status page when Azure has an outage | Azure Service Health alerts for your services and regions |
| Google's SRE book on SLOs | Checkout's SLI, SLO, error budget and burn-rate alerts |

## Guiding your team

> [!TEAM]
> In the weekly alert review, ask:
>
> 1. **Which alerts fired, and which were actionable?** Delete or tune the rest.
> 2. **Does every paging alert describe user impact, and link to a runbook?**
> 3. **What are our SLOs, and how much error budget is left this month?**
> 4. **Who receives Service Health alerts for our regions?**
> 5. **How many times was the on-call engineer paged out of hours, and was each one worth it?**

## Key takeaways

- An alert rule watches a metric, a log query or the activity log and fires with a severity; action groups decide who is notified and what runs.
- Page on symptoms users feel; send causes such as CPU to dashboards and tickets.
- Define SLIs and SLOs for key journeys, alert on how fast the error budget burns, and let the remaining budget decide between features and reliability work.
- Route Azure Service Health alerts to on-call; use alert processing rules for maintenance windows, with end dates.
- On-call works with rotations, runbooks in every alert, mitigation before diagnosis, weekly alert reviews and blameless post-incident reviews.
