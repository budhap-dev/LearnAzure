## Why this matters

> [!SCENARIO] "We scaled the app and nothing happened"
> Ana's Node API has been on [App Service](gl:app-service) for six weeks. Two things are bothering the team.
>
> First, the bill. There are four apps in the subscription and the invoice shows three App Service plans, one of them costing £120 a month with nothing deployed on it - somebody created it while experimenting and never deleted it.
>
> Second, last Thursday the API fell over under load. Ana opened the app in the portal, found "Scale out", set it to four instances, and nothing changed - because the app she was looking at was on a Free plan she had forgotten about, and scaling the *app* is not a thing you can do. Then a 5pm deploy took the site down for six minutes, because the release went straight to production with no slot to warm up first.
>
> Three problems, one root cause: nobody on the team could say what an App Service plan actually is.

## The plan versus the app

This is the single most misunderstood thing in Azure PaaS, so say it slowly.

| | [App Service plan](gl:app-service-plan) | App Service app |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | The compute: an OS, a region, a pricing tier and an instance count | Your code, its settings, its domain and its slots |
| What it costs | **Everything.** Billed per instance, per hour, whether or not anything is deployed | Nothing by itself |
| What scales | Instance count and tier live here | Nothing scales at the app level |
| How many | One plan can host many apps | Each app belongs to exactly one plan |

==The App Service plan is the thing you size, scale and pay for; apps and their slots are tenants living on it.==

That explains Ana's empty £120 plan: an App Service plan bills for the compute it reserves, not for the code on it. It also explains the failed scale-out - the dial is on the plan.

Putting several apps on one plan is genuinely good for cost: five small internal APIs on one P1v3 cost the same as one. It is equally bad for blast radius. They share CPU, memory and instance count, so a memory leak in the reporting app takes the checkout API with it. The rule most teams settle on is: production customer-facing apps get their own plan; internal and non-production apps share.

## Tiers

Figures below are illustrative Linux list prices and change by region and month; check the [pricing calculator](gl:azure-pricing-calculator) before you quote one.

| Tier | [SLA](gl:sla) | Custom domain + TLS | [Slots](gl:deployment-slot) and autoscale | Zone redundancy + [VNet integration](gl:vnet-integration) | Rough price |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Free / Shared** | **None** | Free: no. Shared: domain only | No | No | £0 - a few pounds |
| **Basic (B1-B3)** | 99.95% | Yes | Slots no, manual scale to 3 | No | ~£12-50/month |
| **Standard (S1-S3)** | 99.95% | Yes | 5 slots, autoscale to 10 | VNet yes, zones no | ~£55-220/month |
| **Premium v3 (P0v3-P3v3)** | 99.95% | Yes | 20 slots, autoscale to 30 | Both | ~£75-600/month |
| **Isolated v2** ([App Service Environment](gl:app-service-environment)) | 99.95% | Yes | 20 slots, autoscale to 100 | Both, single-tenant | ~£800+/month |

Two things to take from that table. Free and Shared carry **no SLA at all** - fine for a spike, never for anything a customer touches ([lesson 1.7](lesson:1.7) explains why an SLA-less tier poisons a [composite SLA](gl:composite-sla)). And the jump most teams make is Basic to Premium v3, skipping Standard, because slots plus [zone redundancy](gl:zone-redundant) is what production actually needs and Premium v3's price-performance is better than Standard's.

## Scaling

Two words that are not interchangeable:

- **[Scale up](gl:scale-up)**: change the plan's tier, so every instance is bigger. More RAM for a fat Node process, or a jump to Premium v3 to unlock slots and zones.
- **[Scale out](gl:scale-out)**: change the plan's instance count, manually or with [autoscale](gl:autoscale) rules on CPU, memory, HTTP queue length, or a schedule.

Scale out first for web traffic; scale up when one request needs more memory or CPU than an instance has. Billing is per instance, so scaling out from two to six instances multiplies that part of the bill by three. Give every autoscale rule a maximum, and set the schedule you actually need - most retail traffic wants two instances at 03:00 and six at 19:00, not six all day.

Code gets there by zip deploy, a container image, or a GitHub Actions or Azure Pipelines workflow - the last is what you want, because it is repeatable. **Run-from-package** mounts the zip read-only instead of unpacking it, which makes deploys atomic and start-up faster.

A **[deployment slot](gl:deployment-slot)** is a second copy of the app on the same plan with its own hostname. The release becomes: deploy to `staging`, smoke-test the real thing, swap. The swap warms the staging instances first, then exchanges the routing, so nobody sees a cold start - and swapping back is your rollback.

> [!WARNING] The two ways a swap ruins an evening
> **Swapping cold.** If the app has no warm-up path, or `WEBSITE_SWAP_WARMUP_PING_PATH` points at something that returns 404, production receives traffic on instances that have not JIT-compiled or filled a cache. Give staging real traffic before you swap.
>
> **A setting that is not sticky.** [App settings](gl:app-setting) travel with the code during a swap unless you mark them as **[slot settings](gl:slot-setting)** (sticky). Forget to mark the database connection string, and after the swap production is happily writing to the staging database. Mark every environment-specific setting sticky, and check the swap preview before confirming.

## Configuration

| Concern | How App Service does it | The gotcha |
|---|---|---|
| Environment variables | App settings, injected into the process | Anyone with Reader on the app can read them |
| Secrets | Key Vault references in an app setting, read with a [managed identity](gl:managed-identity) | Needs the identity granted access first ([lesson 1.8](lesson:1.8)) |
| Connection strings | A typed settings section; can be slot-sticky | Not sticky by default - see above |
| Idle apps | **[Always On](gl:always-on)** keeps the process alive | Off by default below Basic; without it the first request after 20 idle minutes is slow |
| Bad instances | **[Health check](gl:health-check)** path polled per instance | An unhealthy instance is removed from rotation, not restarted for free |
| Debugging | **[Kudu](gl:kudu)** / SCM at `<app>.scm.azurewebsites.net` | The console, log stream and file system live here - lock it down |

Background work has a home too: **[WebJobs](gl:webjob)** run continuous or scheduled processes alongside the web app on the same plan. Handy, but they compete for the same CPU as your requests.

## Networking, briefly

Three levers, all covered properly in [Module 4](module:4). **VNet integration** puts the app's *outbound* traffic into your virtual network, so it can reach a private database. **Private endpoints** make the *inbound* side private, so the app is not on the public internet. **App Service Environment** (Isolated v2) puts the whole thing on single-tenant hardware inside your network - real isolation, real money, and rarely the right first answer.

## What App Service is bad at

- **Long-running background work.** Requests time out at around 230 seconds regardless of your code. Put that work in [Azure Functions](gl:azure-functions) or a queue worker.
- **Unusual runtimes or OS-level dependencies.** If you need a specific system library or a kernel module, you are into containers ([lesson 2.5](lesson:2.5)) or a [virtual machine](gl:virtual-machine) ([lesson 2.1](lesson:2.1)).
- **Very high scale-out.** Per-instance pricing that is a bargain at three instances is painful at forty.
- **Persistent local disk.** Instances come and go and the file system is shared and slow. State belongs in a database or a [storage account](gl:storage-account).

## Map it to what you know

| You already run | On App Service |
|---|---|
| An IIS server, or Nginx plus pm2 on a box you patch | The plan: same job, Microsoft patches the OS and the runtime |
| `.env` files per environment | App settings; secrets as Key Vault references |
| `pm2 reload` for a zero-downtime restart | A slot swap, which also gives you a tested rollback |
| Blue-green by flipping a load balancer | Deployment slots - the same idea, one button |
| A load balancer health check on `/healthz` | The health check path, per instance, built in |
| A cron job on the web server | WebJobs, or better, a Function on a timer trigger |

## Guiding your team

> [!TEAM]
> In the design review, ask:
>
> 1. **How many plans do we have, and what is on each?** Empty plans and forgotten Free plans are the two most common wasted lines on an App Service bill.
> 2. **Which apps share a plan, and are we happy they share a fate?** Customer-facing production gets its own.
> 3. **What tier, and what did it buy us?** The honest answer is usually "slots and zone redundancy", which means Premium v3.
> 4. **Which settings are sticky?** Walk the list out loud before the first swap, not after.
> 5. **Is Always On on and is there a health check path?** Two checkboxes that remove a whole class of "it was slow the first time" tickets.

## Key takeaways

- The App Service plan is the sized, billed compute; apps and slots are tenants on it and cost nothing extra by themselves. An empty plan still bills.
- Apps sharing a plan share CPU, memory and instance count - cheap, but one bad app can take the others down.
- Free and Shared have no SLA. Production usually means Premium v3, because that is where slots plus zone redundancy live.
- Scale up changes the tier, scale out changes the instance count; both are set on the plan, and scaling out multiplies the bill.
- Deploy to a staging slot, warm it, swap - and mark every environment-specific setting as a slot setting or production will pick up staging's database.
- App Service is wrong for long-running jobs, exotic runtimes, persistent local disk and very high instance counts.
