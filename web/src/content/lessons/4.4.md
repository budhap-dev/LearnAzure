## Why this matters

> [!SCENARIO] Three identity stories in one week
> On Monday, the finance director's mailbox starts sending invoices to "new bank details". His password was reused from a breached site, and nothing asked for a second factor.
>
> On Wednesday, Dev finds that a contractor who left three months ago can still sign in to the Azure portal. Nobody knew who had invited him, or to what.
>
> On Friday, Ana asks where the shop's customers should sign in once the React Native app gets accounts. She proposes adding them to the staff admin portal's `users` table, which already holds 40 staff with bcrypt hashes and a "forgot password" email Ana wrote herself.
>
> Priya realises the shop has no identity design at all, just places where passwords happen to be stored.

## The idea: one identity provider, many apps

[Microsoft Entra ID](gl:microsoft-entra-id) is an **[identity provider](gl:identity-provider)**: a directory of identities in your [tenant](gl:tenant) plus the service that proves who someone is and hands their app a signed token saying so. Azure, Microsoft 365 and your own apps can all trust it, so one account, one password policy and one "leaver" switch cover everything.

The directory holds four kinds of identity:

| Identity | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **User** | A person in your organisation | priya@shop.co.uk |
| **[Guest user](gl:guest-user)** | A person from outside, signing in with their own organisation's account | The contractor, invited through B2B collaboration |
| **Group** | A set of users that permissions are given to, so people change and rules do not | `grp-catalogue-editors` |
| **Application** | Your app or a third-party one, represented by an app registration and a service principal | The admin portal, the Node API |

Keep one distinction straight. **Authentication** is "who are you?", and Entra ID answers it. **Authorisation** is "what may you do?", and your app or Azure [RBAC](gl:role-based-access-control) answers it, using what the token says.

The conversation between app and Entra ID uses **[OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect](gl:oauth-2-0-and-openid-connect)**. OpenID Connect signs the user in and returns an **ID token** for the front end. OAuth 2.0 issues an **access token** - a signed JWT - that the front end sends to an API, which checks the signature, the issuer and the audience before trusting any claim in it.

## App registrations and service principals

To trust Entra ID, an app is registered. The **[app registration](gl:app-registration)** is the app's global definition: its client ID, where tokens may be sent (redirect URIs), the credentials it proves itself with, the permissions it asks for, and the **app roles** it defines, such as `Orders.Refund`. The **[service principal](gl:service-principal)** is that app's presence in a particular tenant - the thing you assign users to, grant permissions to and can disable. In the portal it appears under *Enterprise applications*.

A front end signs users in and needs no secret. An API, or any code acting as itself, must prove its identity. There are three ways, from worst to best:

1. **A client secret** - a password with an expiry date, at most two years away. When it expires at 3am the integration stops, and it is always sitting in some config file.
2. **A certificate** - better, still something to store and renew.
3. **No secret at all** - a [managed identity](gl:managed-identity) inside Azure, or workload identity federation for GitHub Actions and other clouds ([lesson 4.5](lesson:4.5)).

Give permissions to **groups mapped to app roles**, not to individuals. The token then carries `roles: ["Orders.Refund"]` and the API checks the role, not a list of names.

## Protecting the sign-in itself

==Multifactor authentication stops the great majority of account takeovers, and it is the cheapest security control you will ever deploy.== **[Multifactor authentication](gl:multifactor-authentication)** (MFA) asks for something beyond the password: the Authenticator app, a passkey, a hardware key. Microsoft reports it blocks more than 99% of compromise attempts, and now requires it to sign in to the Azure portal. Prefer **phishing-resistant** methods - passkeys and FIDO2 keys - for admins, because text messages and push approvals can be phished or spammed.

Two ways to turn it on:

- **Security defaults**: free, one switch. MFA for everyone, legacy protocols blocked. Right for a small tenant with no Entra ID P1 licences.
- **[Conditional Access](gl:conditional-access)**: rules of the form *if this user, this app, this location, this device or this risk, then require MFA, require a managed device, or block*. It needs Entra ID P1, about $6 per user per month, or P2 at about $9, which adds risk-based sign-in and PIM.

Every tenant with Conditional Access needs two **[emergency access accounts](gl:emergency-access-account)**: cloud-only, excluded from every policy, with long random passwords or FIDO2 keys kept in a safe, and alerted on every use. They exist for the day a policy locks everyone else out.

## Staff, partners and customers are different tenants

The contractor is a **guest**: he signs in with his own company's account, you control what he can reach, and an **access review** or an expiry date removes him when the work ends. Nobody should hold a separate username and password in your directory.

Customers never go in the workforce tenant. **[Microsoft Entra External ID](gl:microsoft-entra-external-id)** runs a separate *external* tenant for consumer sign-up and sign-in: email one-time codes, Google or Apple sign-in, your own branding, and self-service password reset you did not have to write. The first 50,000 monthly active users are free. It replaces Azure AD B2C, which no longer takes new customers.

## In practice

Priya's plan: the admin portal becomes a single-tenant app registration with app roles `Catalogue.Editor` and `Orders.Refund`, assigned to security groups, and the home-grown `users` table is deleted. The React app uses MSAL.js to sign in; the Node API validates access tokens with a maintained library rather than hand-written JWT code. Conditional Access requires MFA for everyone, phishing-resistant MFA and a managed device for admins, and blocks legacy authentication. The contractor is removed; future contractors are guests with an expiry. Customers go to an External ID tenant.

**What it costs.** Entra ID Free covers users, groups, app registrations and security defaults. P1 at about $6 per user per month adds Conditional Access and group-based app assignment; P2 at about $9 adds risk-based policies, PIM and access reviews. For forty staff, P1 is roughly $240 a month. External ID is free up to 50,000 monthly active customers.

**What breaks.**

- **The expired client secret.** The integration dies on a date written in a portal nobody watches. Prefer managed identity and federation; if a secret is unavoidable, alert before expiry.
- **Wrong audience.** The API rejects a token meant for Microsoft Graph, or the front end sends its ID token to the API. Access tokens are for APIs; ID tokens are for the app that signed in.
- **Groups in tokens.** A user in more than 200 groups gets no groups claim, just a pointer. Use app roles.
- **A policy that locks everyone out.** A new Conditional Access rule applied to "all users" on a Friday. Test in report-only mode first, and keep the emergency accounts excluded.
- **User consent.** Staff grant a third-party app access to their mailbox with one click. Restrict consent to verified publishers and low-risk permissions.

## Map it to what you know

| You already know | On Entra ID |
|---|---|
| On-premises Active Directory | Entra ID, but no OUs, Group Policy or Kerberos; Entra Connect syncs the two for hybrid |
| IIS Windows authentication on the intranet | OpenID Connect sign-in with single sign-on |
| ASP.NET Identity's users table | Entra ID for staff, External ID for customers - no password hashes of your own |
| `passport-jwt` verifying a token in Express | The same idea: verify issuer, audience and signature against Entra's published keys |
| Auth0, Firebase Auth or Cognito | Microsoft Entra External ID |
| A service account with a password that never changes | A service principal - and better, a managed identity |

## Guiding your team

> [!TEAM]
> For any app or integration, ask:
>
> 1. **Who is the identity provider, and are we storing any passwords ourselves?** The answer to the second part should be no.
> 2. **Is MFA enforced for everyone, and phishing-resistant for admins?** Security defaults at the very least.
> 3. **Where are the break-glass accounts, and who is alerted when they are used?**
> 4. **Does this integration use a client secret? When does it expire, and why is it not a managed identity?**
> 5. **How are leavers and contractors removed?** Name the process, not the person.

## Key takeaways

- Entra ID is the identity provider for staff, apps and Azure itself: it authenticates, issues signed tokens, and leaves authorisation to your app and RBAC.
- OpenID Connect signs users in (ID token); OAuth 2.0 gives APIs access tokens, which must be checked for signature, issuer and audience.
- An app registration defines an app; its service principal is that app in your tenant. Prefer no secret at all to certificates, and certificates to client secrets.
- MFA is the highest-value control there is. Security defaults for free, Conditional Access with P1, phishing-resistant methods for admins, and two emergency access accounts.
- Contractors are guests with an expiry; customers belong in a separate External ID tenant, never in the workforce directory.
