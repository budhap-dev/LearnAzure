## Why this matters

> [!SCENARIO] "Put 100% uptime in the contract"
> Priya's retailer is signing a wholesale partner who will place orders through the shop's API. The partner's legal team sends a draft contract with one line that stops everything: "Supplier guarantees 100% availability of the ordering service." The commercial director wants to sign it. Priya's tech lead, Marcus, says nobody can promise that, not even Microsoft.
>
> Priya needs to walk into the negotiation with three things: what 99.9% actually allows, what the shop's real number is once every service in the chain is counted, and what it would cost to add another nine.

Reliability has a precise vocabulary, and stakeholders who hear "99.9%" tend to hear "basically always". This lesson gives you the arithmetic and the words to have that conversation honestly.

## What an SLA is - and is not

A **[service-level agreement](gl:sla)** is Microsoft's published commitment for a service: a monthly uptime percentage and what happens if it is missed. The "what happens" is a **[service credit](gl:service-credit)**: a percentage off that month's bill for that service, which you have to claim. ==An SLA is a financial promise, not an engineering guarantee - it tells you what Microsoft will refund, not what will not fail.==

Three details matter in the contract conversation:

- SLAs are per service, per month, and only apply to configurations that qualify. A single App Service instance on a Free or Shared tier has **no SLA**. A single VM without premium disks has a lower SLA than two VMs in an availability set or across zones.
- A credit is capped at the spend on that service. If a two-hour outage costs the shop £50,000 in orders, the credit might be £30.
- Your own code, your deployments and your configuration mistakes are not covered. Most real downtime is self-inflicted.

## Nines into minutes

| Availability | Downtime per month | Downtime per year | What it feels like |
|---|---|---|---|
| **99%** | 7 h 18 min | 3.65 days | Nearly a working day a month; fine for an internal tool |
| **99.9%** | 43.8 min | 8.77 h | The usual PaaS baseline; a bad afternoon a year |
| **99.95%** | 21.9 min | 4.38 h | App Service, most single-region PaaS |
| **99.99%** | 4.4 min | 52.6 min | Needs zone redundancy; one short incident a year |
| **99.999%** | 26 s | 5.3 min | Multi-region, automated failover, serious money |

Each extra nine is roughly ten times less downtime and, in practice, more than ten times the engineering. The first table row to show a stakeholder is 99.9%: forty-four minutes a month is well within the SLA and would still be a headline if it hit the checkout on Black Friday.

The **[composite SLA](gl:composite-sla)** is what you can actually stand behind, and it is always lower than the weakest service. Marcus's answer to "100%" is: "Today we can commit to 99.9%, our real number is around 99.93%, and every extra nine roughly doubles the infrastructure."

Redundancy **in parallel** works the other way: two independent copies both have to fail. With two instances at 99.95%, the chance both are down is 0.0005 × 0.0005, so availability becomes 1 − 0.00000025. That is why [redundancy](gl:redundancy) is the main lever for adding a nine, and why it costs roughly double.

## RPO, RTO, and the three things people confuse

When something does fail, two numbers define the business impact:

- **[RPO](gl:rpo)** (recovery point objective): how much data you can afford to lose, measured in time. An RPO of 15 minutes means backups or replication must be no more than 15 minutes behind.
- **[RTO](gl:rto)** (recovery time objective): how long you can afford to be down. An RTO of 1 hour means the service must be back within an hour of the failure.

For the shop, Priya sets RPO 15 minutes and RTO 1 hour for the ordering API. Azure SQL's point-in-time restore and geo-replication meet the RPO comfortably; the RTO is met only if failover is rehearsed. Three terms that get blurred:

| Term | Question it answers | Typical Azure mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **[High availability](gl:high-availability)** | Can the service survive a component failing *without* an outage? | Multiple instances, availability zones, zone-redundant databases |
| **[Disaster recovery](gl:disaster-recovery)** | Can we recover in another region if the whole region is lost? | Geo-replication, region pairs, Site Recovery, rehearsed failover |
| **Backup** | Can we get yesterday's data back after corruption or deletion? | Point-in-time restore, Backup vaults, long-term retention |

Backup is not disaster recovery: a nightly backup gives an RPO of 24 hours and an RTO of however long a restore takes. High availability is not disaster recovery either: two zones in one region do not survive that region being unavailable.

## Redundancy levels and what they cost

| Level | Protects against | Cost implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Instance** (two or more copies in one data centre) | A VM or process dying, a host being patched | Roughly 2x compute |
| **Zone** (copies across availability zones in a region) | A data centre losing power or cooling | Small premium on zone-redundant PaaS; 2-3x on VMs |
| **Region** (a second deployment in a paired region) | A regional outage or disaster | 2x everything, plus data replication egress and the cost of practising failover |

==Reliability is a spectrum you pay for: pick the point on it that the business can justify, write that number down, and design to it.==

## The Well-Architected Framework

The **[Azure Well-Architected Framework](gl:well-architected-framework)** is Microsoft's checklist for the trade-offs above, organised into five pillars. One question per pillar is enough for a design review:

| Pillar | The question to ask |
|---|---|
| **Reliability** | What is the RPO, RTO and target SLA, and what is the composite SLA of the design? |
| **Security** | Where are secrets stored, and what is the blast radius if one leaks? |
| **Cost Optimization** | What switches off, what is reserved, and who reviews the bill ([lesson 1.6](lesson:1.6))? |
| **Operational Excellence** | How does a deployment go out, and how do we know it worked? |
| **Performance Efficiency** | What scales, on what signal, and have we load-tested it? |

![Advisor](az:advisor) [Azure Advisor](gl:azure-advisor) checks a subscription against the same five pillars and reports specific findings, such as a VM without zone redundancy or a database without geo-backup. It is the cheapest design review you will ever get.

## In practice

The negotiation ends with 99.9% in the contract, credits mirroring Microsoft's, and an explicit list of exclusions for scheduled maintenance. Internally, the team writes down RPO 15 minutes and RTO 1 hour, moves the API to two zone-redundant instances, and books a failover rehearsal every quarter. Total added cost: roughly 40% on the API tier. Total added cost of the "100%" version: unbounded.

## Map it to what you know

| You already know | The reliability equivalent |
|---|---|
| SQL Server AlwaysOn, or log shipping every 15 minutes | RPO and RTO: how far behind the replica is, how fast you can switch |
| A React error boundary rendering a fallback | Graceful degradation: one component fails, the page stays up |
| Retries with back-off in an HTTP client | [Transient fault](gl:transient-fault) handling - the platform's first line of [resiliency](gl:resiliency) |
| A chain of promises where one rejection fails the whole `await` | Composite SLA in series: every link must succeed |
| `Promise.any` resolving if either call succeeds | Redundancy in parallel: both must fail before you do |

## Guiding your team

> [!TEAM]
> Whenever "uptime" appears in a document, ask:
>
> 1. **Which number, and is it measured or promised?** SLA, composite SLA and observed uptime are three different things.
> 2. **What is the composite SLA of the design?** Multiply the chain before anyone commits to a figure.
> 3. **What are the RPO and RTO, and when did we last rehearse them?** An unrehearsed failover is a hope.
> 4. **Which tier has no SLA?** Free, shared and single-instance resources do not belong in the chain.
> 5. **What does the next nine cost?** Put the price next to the promise.

> [!TIP] Exam link
> AZ-900 asks you to describe SLAs, service credits, and the factors that affect them, and to name the five Well-Architected pillars. Expect a question that gives two or three service SLAs and asks for the composite; multiply them and round down.

## Key takeaways

- An SLA is a financial commitment with service credits, not a guarantee; free and single-instance tiers have none.
- 99.9% allows about 44 minutes of downtime a month; each extra nine cuts that by ten and costs far more than ten times the effort.
- Services in series multiply their SLAs downward; redundancy in parallel raises availability by 1 − (1−a)(1−b).
- RPO is how much data you can lose; RTO is how long you can be down. Backup, high availability and disaster recovery answer different questions.
- The five Well-Architected pillars - Reliability, Security, Cost Optimization, Operational Excellence, Performance Efficiency - are the design-review checklist, and Advisor runs it for you.
