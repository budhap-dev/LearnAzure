## Why this matters

> [!SCENARIO] "Are we well-architected?" - and 212 recommendations
> A year of change later, the shop has a new CTO. Her first question is the one the insurer asked too: *are we well-architected?* Dev opens Azure Advisor and finds 212 recommendations. Someone suggests filling in Microsoft's online assessment for the whole Azure estate, and a spreadsheet of findings starts to grow.
>
> Priya stops it. A list of 212 items is not a plan, and "the whole estate" is not something a team can reason about in one sitting. She wants a review of one workload, with the people who run it, that ends with ten risks, each with an owner, and a record of the trade-offs the team chose on purpose.

## What a Well-Architected review is

The [Well-Architected Framework](gl:well-architected-framework) from [lesson 1.7](lesson:1.7) names five pillars: **Reliability, Security, Cost Optimization, Operational Excellence and Performance Efficiency**. A **[Well-Architected Review](gl:well-architected-review)** applies them to one **workload** - the set of components that together deliver one business capability - and asks, pillar by pillar, *where are we exposed, and what will we do about it?*

Three tools feed it:

- **The Azure Well-Architected Review assessment**: Microsoft's free online questionnaire per pillar. It produces a score and linked guidance, and is best used as a prompt list, not a verdict.
- **[Azure Advisor](gl:azure-advisor)**: continuous, automated recommendations from your actual resources, grouped by the same pillars - rightsizing, missing zone redundancy, idle resources, security findings - with an **Advisor score**.
- **Service guides**: Well-Architected guidance for each Azure service, such as App Service, Azure SQL and Service Bus, listing its specific reliability, security and cost settings.

==A review is a conversation about trade-offs for one workload, not a questionnaire to complete; its output is a short, owned list of risks and a written record of the decisions.==

## Scope one workload

Priya picks **checkout**: Front Door, the Node API, Azure SQL, Service Bus, the warehouse integration, Key Vault, and the monitoring around them. The team walks into the review with five inputs: the architecture diagram, the SLOs and error budget ([lesson 6.4](lesson:6.4)), last quarter's costs, the incident list, and an Advisor export filtered to the checkout resource groups.

## The pillars, applied

| Pillar | The question that matters | Shop's finding | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reliability** | Do we meet our RTO and RPO, and have we proven it? | Yes for checkout - drilled at 22 minutes ([6.5](lesson:6.5)). Redis is not zone-redundant. | Accept for now: the cache rebuilds itself. Revisit if it becomes session-critical. |
| **Security** | Does the workload meet the minimum bar ([4.8](lesson:4.8))? | Service Bus Standard has no private endpoint - a recorded exception that expires next month ([5.2](lesson:5.2)). | Decide: Premium, or renew the exception with a reason. |
| **Cost Optimization** | Are we paying for things we do not use? | Dev App Service plans run 24/7 on production sizes; no SQL reservation. | Scale dev down out of hours; buy a one-year vCore reservation for prod. |
| **Operational Excellence** | Can we change it safely and run it at 3am? | The warehouse VM is configured by hand; its runbook is out of date. | Move its configuration to code ([Module 7](module:7)); refresh the runbook. |
| **Performance Efficiency** | Do we know where it breaks? | Load-tested to 3x last peak; catalogue N+1 fixed ([6.3](lesson:6.3)). | Add a p95 regression check to the release pipeline. |

The pillars pull against each other. Warm standby buys reliability with money. Private endpoints buy security with cost and complexity. Sampling saves money and loses some visibility. The review's job is to make those trades on purpose and write them down, not to max out every pillar.

## Failure mode analysis

The most valuable hour of the review goes on **[failure mode analysis](gl:failure-mode-analysis)**: for each component of the workload, what can fail, what the user sees, how you would know, and what limits the damage.

| Component | Failure | User impact | Detected by | Mitigation | Residual risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Payment provider | Down or slow | Cannot pay | SLO burn alert | Circuit breaker; friendly retry message | Lost sales for the duration |
| Azure SQL (UK South) | Region lost | Checkout down | Service Health, availability tests | Failover group to UK West, 22-minute RTO | Under a minute of orders, recovered by the outbox |
| Service Bus | Namespace unavailable | None immediately | Dead-letter and age alerts | Outbox holds events until it returns | Delayed warehouse updates |
| Key Vault | Throttled or unreachable | Start-up failures | App start errors | References resolved at start-up and cached | New instances cannot start during the outage |
| Front Door | Global incident | Site unreachable | Availability tests | None beyond Front Door's own global redundancy - accepted | Rare but total; documented and accepted |

Writing down the row with *no mitigation, accepted* is as important as the others. It is a decision, not an oversight.

## Recording decisions

Trade-offs that live only in someone's head get re-argued every six months. An **[architecture decision record](gl:architecture-decision-record)** (ADR) is a short file in the repository: the context, the decision, its consequences, and what would make you revisit it.

```
# ADR-014: Service Bus stays on Standard for checkout

Date: 2026-09-26
Status: Accepted

Context: The security baseline asks for private endpoints on data services.
Service Bus private endpoints require Premium (~$680/month vs ~$10).

Decision: Stay on Standard with Entra-only auth and SAS keys disabled.

Consequences: Service Bus keeps a public endpoint, restricted by identity only.

Revisit when: messages carry personal data, or monthly volume exceeds
Standard's comfortable range, or by 2027-03-31.
```

## Running the review

- **Before**: pick the workload, gather the five inputs, and triage Advisor. The shop's 212 recommendations became 14 to act on, 30 dismissed with a reason recorded in Advisor, and the rest parked as not relevant to checkout.
- **During**: two hours with engineering, operations, product and someone who owns the budget. Walk each pillar's key question, then the failure mode table.
- **After**: the top ten risks go into the backlog with owners and dates, ADRs are written for every trade-off accepted, and the next review is booked - quarterly, and after any major change.

## In practice

The shop's first review took a morning. It produced ten risks, four ADRs, one accepted single point of failure written down in plain words, and a monthly saving of about $1,100 from dev scaling and a SQL reservation. That paid for the standby region from 6.5. The CTO's question gets a real answer: *checkout is reviewed; here are its risks, their owners and the trade-offs we chose; the next review is in January.*

**What breaks.**

- **Reviewing the whole estate.** Nobody can hold it in their head; findings stay generic. One workload at a time.
- **The questionnaire as the goal.** A score without a conversation changes nothing.
- **Chasing Advisor score.** Some recommendations do not fit your workload; dismiss them with a reason rather than implementing everything.
- **Findings without owners.** A list in a slide deck is forgotten by the next sprint.
- **Once and never again.** Architectures drift. Review quarterly and after every major change.

## Map it to what you know

| You already know | In a Well-Architected review |
|---|---|
| A code review | The same idea for architecture: structured, collaborative, and it ends in changes |
| An architecture review board | A lighter, team-run review per workload, repeated |
| FMEA from engineering or manufacturing | Failure mode analysis per component |
| SonarQube findings on a pull request | Azure Advisor recommendations - triage, do not blindly fix |
| Decision notes lost in email threads | Architecture decision records in the repository |

## Guiding your team

> [!TEAM]
> For the next review, ask:
>
> 1. **Which single workload are we reviewing, and who from product and finance is in the room?**
> 2. **For each component, what fails, what do users see, how do we know, and what limits the damage?**
> 3. **Which trade-offs are we accepting, and where are they written down?**
> 4. **Which Advisor recommendations did we dismiss, and why?**
> 5. **Who owns each of the top ten risks, by when - and when is the next review?**

## Key takeaways

- A Well-Architected Review applies the five pillars to one workload with the people who run it; the assessment and Advisor are inputs, not the output.
- Scope tightly, bring evidence - diagram, SLOs, costs, incidents, triaged Advisor findings - and walk each pillar's key question.
- Failure mode analysis per component, including the risks you accept, is the most valuable part.
- The pillars trade against each other; record every deliberate trade-off as an architecture decision record.
- End with a short, owned risk list and the next review date. Repeat quarterly and after major changes.
