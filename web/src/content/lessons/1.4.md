## Why this matters

> [!SCENARIO] Who deleted the tracking database?
> Dana is head of platform at Northline Freight. Three teams - Bookings, Tracking and Billing - share one Azure account that was set up in an afternoon two years ago. Everything sits in a single subscription: dev, test and prod, side by side. Every developer is an Owner, because that was easiest.
>
> On Tuesday a contractor on the Tracking team "cleaned up some old test stuff" and deleted the production tracking database. Restoring it took four hours. On Wednesday finance asked Dana to split the monthly bill by team, and she could not. On Thursday the security auditor asked who can delete what, and the honest answer was "everyone, everything".

Nothing about Northline's code was wrong. The **structure** around it was. This lesson is the map you need before you create anything else.

## The hierarchy

Azure arranges everything into a tree with five levels. Each level is a [scope](gl:scope): a place where access, policy and cost can be controlled, and where those controls flow downwards.

What each level is *for*:

| Level | It is the boundary for | Northline example |
|---|---|---|
| [Tenant](gl:tenant) | **Identity.** One [Microsoft Entra ID](gl:microsoft-entra-id) directory of users, groups and apps. Everything below trusts it. | `northline.com` - the same sign-in as Microsoft 365 |
| [Management group](gl:management-group) | **Governance across subscriptions.** Policy and role assignments set here apply to every subscription underneath. | `mg-northline`, with "UK regions only" applied once |
| [Subscription](gl:subscription) | **Billing, quotas and a policy edge.** One invoice line, its own limits (VM cores, IPs), a natural place to say "prod rules apply here". | `sub-prod` and `sub-nonprod` |
| [Resource group](gl:resource-group) | **Lifecycle.** Things that are deployed, updated and deleted together. Deleting the group deletes everything in it. | `rg-tracking-prod` |
| [Resource](gl:resource) | **The thing itself** - a web app, a database, a storage account. | The Tracking API on App Service |

==Access and policy are set at a scope and inherited by everything beneath it, so put each rule at the highest level where it is true for everyone below.==

## Inheritance: RBAC and Policy flow down

Two systems ride this tree; Module 4 covers both in depth:

- [Role-based access control](gl:role-based-access-control) (RBAC) answers *who can do what*. A role (Reader, Contributor, Owner) is assigned to a user or group at a scope. Assign Reader at the management group and everything beneath inherits it.
- [Azure Policy](gl:azure-policy) answers *what is allowed to exist*: "only UK regions", "every resource carries a cost-centre tag". Assign it once at the top and new subscriptions are born compliant.

Northline's fix is mostly about scopes. Developers get Contributor on `sub-nonprod` and Reader on `sub-prod`; a deployment pipeline changes prod. The contractor could never have seen the delete button.

## Choosing subscriptions

A subscription is free to create, so the question is how many, and split by what.

| Strategy | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Per environment** (prod, nonprod) | Clean blast radius, simple policy ("prod is strict"), prod cost visible at a glance | Team costs need tags to separate |
| **Per team** | Bill lines match the org chart, teams own their quotas | Prod and dev share a subscription unless you also split by environment |
| **Per workload** | Best isolation, quotas never collide, easy to hand over or decommission | Many subscriptions to govern; management groups become essential |

Most organisations combine them: a management group per team with a prod and a nonprod subscription inside each. The Cloud Adoption Framework calls the whole arrangement a **landing zone**; know the shape early so first choices do not fight it.

## Resource groups, names, tags and locks

A resource group holds things with the *same lifecycle*. The Tracking API, its database and its storage account go together; a shared virtual network that four teams depend on does not. A group has a [region](lesson:1.3) of its own, but that is only where its metadata lives - the resources inside can be anywhere.

Names cannot be changed after creation, so agree a [naming convention](gl:naming-convention) first. The Cloud Adoption Framework pattern is `type-workload-environment-region`:

| Resource | Name |
|---|---|
| Resource group | `rg-tracking-prod-uksouth` |
| App Service | `app-tracking-api-prod` |
| SQL server | `sql-tracking-prod-uksouth` |
| Storage account (no hyphens, globally unique) | `sttrackingprod001` |

Names describe *what*. [Tags](gl:tag) describe *whose* and *why*, and they are what cost reports group by:

| Tag | Example | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| `owner` | `tracking-team@northline.com` | Who to ask before touching it |
| `cost-centre` | `CC-4410` | Finance's chargeback question |
| `environment` | `prod` | Filtering, and policies that only bite in prod |
| `workload` | `tracking` | Grouping across resource groups |

Tags are not inherited. Use a policy to require them, or [cost reporting](lesson:1.6) will have gaps within a month.

Finally, a [resource lock](gl:resource-lock) is a belt-and-braces guard on a scope: **CanNotDelete** or **ReadOnly**. A delete lock on `rg-tracking-prod` would have turned Tuesday's incident into a polite error message, even for an Owner - they would have had to remove the lock first, deliberately.

## In practice: the resource ID

Every resource has a [resource ID](gl:resource-id) that is literally its path through the tree:

```text
/subscriptions/3f2c.../resourceGroups/rg-tracking-prod-uksouth/providers/Microsoft.Web/sites/app-tracking-api-prod
```

Read it right to left and you have the resource, its provider, its group and its subscription. You will paste these into role assignments, pipelines and support tickets. [Lesson 1.5](lesson:1.5) explains the `providers/Microsoft.Web` part.

## Map it to what you know

| You already know | The Azure equivalent |
|---|---|
| A monorepo with workspaces, each with its own lint rules inherited from the root | Management groups and subscriptions: rules at the root, inherited by every workspace |
| One `package.json` project: install, build, delete as a unit | A resource group: deploy, update and delete together |
| SQL Server instance → database → schema → table | Subscription → resource group → resource, with permissions granted at any level |
| A Git repo per team with branch protection on `main` | A subscription per team with a locked-down prod scope |
| The full path to a file | The resource ID |

## Guiding your team

> [!TEAM]
> Before the first resource is created, settle these:
>
> 1. **How many subscriptions, split by what?** Prod and nonprod apart is the minimum.
> 2. **What is the highest scope this rule is true at?** Put the RBAC or policy there, not on each resource.
> 3. **What deletes together?** That is your resource group boundary.
> 4. **What is the naming convention and the four mandatory tags?** Write them down; enforce them with policy.
> 5. **Which scopes get a delete lock?** Anything with data in prod.

> [!TIP] Exam link
> AZ-900 asks you to describe the hierarchy (management groups, subscriptions, resource groups, resources), the purpose of tags, and resource locks. Being able to sketch the diagram above and say what each level is *for* covers all of it.

## Key takeaways

- The tree is tenant → management groups → subscriptions → resource groups → resources. Each level is a scope.
- Tenant is identity; management groups are governance across subscriptions; a subscription is a billing, quota and policy boundary; a resource group is a lifecycle boundary.
- RBAC and Policy are set at a scope and inherited downwards. Assign at the highest true scope.
- Split prod from nonprod at the subscription level at minimum; combine per-team and per-environment as you grow.
- Names are permanent and describe what; tags describe who and why; locks stop accidents. Decide all three before day one.
