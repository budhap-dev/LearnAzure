## Why this matters

> [!SCENARIO] "You run the reviews now"
> Two years after lesson 1.1, Northline runs on Azure: the shop, the loyalty programme, the wholesale portal, an AI assistant, and a migrated estate in a governed landing zone. The CTO's last request of this course is simple. Every significant design now goes through a review, and Priya and her engineering managers run them.
>
> Some reviews so far have been rubber stamps. Others turned into two-hour arguments won by whoever talked loudest. None left a record anyone could find a month later.
>
> This lesson is how to run a design review that improves the design, and four scenarios to practise on. Each one draws on several modules.

## How to run a design review

A **[design review](gl:design-review)** is a structured conversation that tests a design against its requirements before it is expensive to change. It is not an exam for the author.

**Before**: the author circulates a short design document, two pages at most:

- The problem and context.
- The **[non-functional requirements](gl:non-functional-requirements)** *as numbers*.
- The closest reference architecture and the deviations from it ([lesson 9.1](lesson:9.1)).
- A diagram.
- Monthly cost and cost per unit.
- The known risks.

Reviewers read it beforehand.

**During**, in 60 minutes:

| Minutes | Step |
|---|---|
| 10 | The author restates the problem and the numbers. No slides beyond the document. |
| 30 | Walk the design through the Well-Architected pillars ([lesson 6.6](lesson:6.6)): reliability, security, cost, operations, performance. |
| 10 | **[Pre-mortem](gl:pre-mortem)**: "It is a year from now and this failed badly. What happened?" |
| 10 | Decisions, actions with owners and dates, and a verdict. |

**After**, three outputs are recorded: an [ADR](gl:architecture-decision-record) for each significant decision, actions in the backlog with owners, and a verdict of *go*, *go with conditions* or *not yet*.

Three habits make the difference.

**Numbers, not adjectives.** "Highly available" cannot be reviewed. "99.9% monthly, RTO one hour, RPO five minutes, p95 under 400 ms at 2,000 requests per second, data in the EU, under $6,000 a month" can.

**Name the trade-offs.** Every **[architecture trade-off](gl:architecture-trade-off)** spends one quality to buy another: a second region buys reliability with cost and complexity; private endpoints buy security with operational effort; a cache buys performance with staleness. A good review makes each trade explicit and agreed, rather than accidental.

**Record deliberate shortcuts.** **[Technical debt](gl:technical-debt)** taken on knowingly, such as a single region for launch or keys instead of managed identity for one partner, goes into a register. Each entry has its cost and the trigger that forces repayment.

==A design review succeeds when its decisions are better and written down: numbers agreed, trade-offs named, ADRs recorded, actions owned.==

## Capstone scenarios

Run each one as a review with your team. The questions are what a good reviewer asks, and the strong design is what the answers should add up to. Every point links back to something in this course.

> [!EXAMPLE] 1. Black Friday at ten times normal traffic
> **Brief**: the shop expects ten times its normal peak for 48 hours. Last year checkout slowed to 12 seconds and the assistant returned errors.
>
> **Ask**: What is the target, in requests per second, at p95? What did the load test show, and where did it break first? What scales automatically, and what hits a fixed limit (SQL tier, Redis size, model TPM quota, API Management units)? What can we switch off to protect checkout?
>
> **A strong design**: load tests at 12 times normal, run weeks ahead ([lesson 6.5](lesson:6.5)). Pre-scaled SQL and Redis tiers, with autoscale rules checked against their maximums. Checkout's slow work moved to queues ([lesson 5.1](lesson:5.1)). The assistant's quota raised through the gateway, with a PTU baseline ([lesson 8.8](lesson:8.8)). Feature flags to shed recommendations and the assistant first. A change freeze, and SLO alerts with a staffed on-call rota ([lesson 6.4](lesson:6.4)).
>
> **The trap**: "It autoscales" as the whole answer, while the database tier is fixed.

> [!EXAMPLE] 2. A partner API for wholesale customers
> **Brief**: fifty wholesale customers will integrate their purchasing systems with Northline's order API.
>
> **Ask**: How do partners authenticate, and how do we revoke one? What stops one partner overwhelming the API? How do we change the API without breaking them? What is private, and what faces the internet?
>
> **A strong design**: API Management products, with each partner using OAuth client credentials under its own identity ([lessons 4.4](lesson:4.4) and [5.5](lesson:5.5)). Rate limits and quotas per partner. Versioned APIs with a deprecation policy and contract tests ([lesson 7.8](lesson:7.8)). A WAF at the edge, and back ends reachable only privately ([lesson 4.3](lesson:4.3)). A published SLO and a status page.
>
> **The trap**: one shared API key for all fifty partners.

> [!EXAMPLE] 3. "The shop must never go down"
> **Brief**: after a regional incident at another retailer, the board asks for the shop never to go down.
>
> **Ask**: What does downtime actually cost per hour? Which journeys matter: browse, checkout, the account area? What RTO and RPO follow from that? What would active-active cost against active-passive, and what does each protect against?
>
> **A strong design**: "never" is translated into numbers the board agrees: checkout RTO one hour, RPO five minutes. Active-passive, with zone redundancy in the primary region ([lesson 6.5](lesson:6.5)), and a failover drilled twice a year. The cost difference is shown against the cost of downtime. An ADR records why active-active was declined.
>
> **The trap**: building active-active for every component, at three times the cost, without asking what an hour of downtime costs.

> [!EXAMPLE] 4. An AI assistant for store staff
> **Brief**: store managers want an assistant that answers questions about HR policies, rotas and stock, and can raise stock transfers.
>
> **Ask**: Who may see which documents, and how does retrieval enforce that? Which actions can it take, and under whose identity? How do we know its answers are right before launch and after? What does a conversation cost, and what caps the spend?
>
> **A strong design**: retrieval filtered by Entra group, so HR content only reaches managers ([lesson 8.5](lesson:8.5)). Tools called with the user's identity, with transfers above a threshold needing approval ([lesson 8.6](lesson:8.6)). An evaluation set with adversarial cases, gating every release ([lesson 8.7](lesson:8.7)). Data zone deployments behind the AI gateway, with token limits per app, and a kill switch ([lesson 8.8](lesson:8.8)).
>
> **The trap**: one index for everyone, and a system prompt that says "don't show HR data to staff".

## In practice

Priya's review practice:

- **Right-sized**: a full review for anything new, anything that crosses team or network boundaries, or anything handling personal data. For smaller changes, a written ADR with one reviewer is enough.
- **Early**: the review happens when the design is two pages, not two sprints in.
- **Shared**: a template in the platform repository, and every review logged with its ADRs and verdict.
- **Rotating chair**: engineering managers take turns chairing, so the skill spreads. Security and the platform team attend when their areas are touched.
- **Closing the loop**: actions are checked at the next review, and critical workloads get a Well-Architected Review every quarter ([lesson 6.6](lesson:6.6)).

**What breaks.**

- **Reviewing at the end**, when changing the design means throwing work away.
- **Adjectives instead of numbers.**
- **The loudest voice deciding**, with no pillars to structure the conversation.
- **No record**, so the same argument happens again next quarter.
- **Actions without owners**, so nothing changes.
- **Every change reviewed with the same weight**, so the process becomes a queue that people route around.

## Map it to what you know

| You already know | In an architecture practice |
|---|---|
| A pull request review | A design review, done before the code exists |
| An RFC process, like React's | The design document and its review |
| A sprint retrospective | A pre-mortem: a retrospective held before anything has gone wrong |
| A bug backlog | A technical debt register with repayment triggers |
| "It works on my machine" | Adjectives instead of NFR numbers |

## Guiding your team

> [!TEAM]
> Ten questions you can ask of any design, one for each part of this course:
>
> 1. **What are the requirements in numbers**: availability, RTO and RPO, latency, load, residency, budget? ([Module 1](module:1))
> 2. **Why this compute and this data store?** ([Modules 2](module:2) and [3](module:3))
> 3. **Whose identity does each call use, and what can reach it from the network?** ([Module 4](module:4))
> 4. **What happens when each dependency is slow or down?** ([Modules 5](module:5) and [6](module:6))
> 5. **How do we know it is healthy, and who gets paged?** ([Module 6](module:6))
> 6. **How does it get deployed, and how do we roll back in minutes?** ([Module 7](module:7))
> 7. **If it uses AI, how is quality evaluated and spend capped?** ([Module 8](module:8))
> 8. **Which reference architecture, and where do we differ?** (lesson 9.1)
> 9. **What does it cost per month and per unit, and who is alerted?** (lesson 9.2)
> 10. **It is a year from now and this failed. Why?**

## Key takeaways

- A design review tests a design against its requirements while it is cheap to change. It is not an exam for the author.
- Come with a short document: non-functional requirements as numbers, the reference architecture and deviations, a diagram, costs and risks.
- In 60 minutes: restate, walk the Well-Architected pillars, run a pre-mortem, then decide.
- The outputs are ADRs, actions with owners and dates, and a verdict. Deliberate shortcuts go into a technical debt register with triggers.
- Name every trade-off, review early, weight the process by risk, and rotate the chair so the skill spreads across the team.
