## Why this matters

> [!SCENARIO] "Where will the data actually be?"
> Priya's retailer has chosen its service models (see [lesson 1.2](lesson:1.2)). The migration plan is on the legal team's desk, and it comes back with one question circled: "Where will customer data physically live?" The company is UK-based, but a third of new orders now come from India, and marketing wants a Mumbai launch next spring.
>
> Dev's answer is "in the cloud". Legal does not accept that. Ana adds a second problem: the React app loads in under a second in London and takes four seconds in Bengaluru. Priya needs to explain where Azure is, how it is laid out, and why that decides both the legal answer and the four seconds.

Azure is not one place. It is physical locations arranged in layers, and every choice about resilience, speed and compliance comes down to which layer you rely on.

## From data centre to geography

Start at the bottom and work up.

- A **[data centre](gl:data-centre)** is a building full of servers. You never pick one directly.
- An **[availability zone](gl:availability-zone)** is one or more data centres with their own power, cooling and networking - close enough to the other zones for a two-millisecond round trip, far enough apart that a fire or power failure takes out only one.
- A **[region](gl:region)** is a set of data centres, usually in three zones, inside a boundary where Azure sells services. This is the unit you choose: every resource is created *in* a region such as UK South. ![Region](az:region)
- A **[geography](gl:geography)** is a market, usually a country or a bloc, holding one or more regions and defining where your data at rest is kept. The United Kingdom, Europe and India are geographies.

Most regions have a **[region pair](gl:region-pair)**: a second region in the same geography, hundreds of miles away. Microsoft sequences planned maintenance so both are never updated at once, and if a whole region goes down, the pair is first in the recovery queue.

==A zone protects you from a building failing; a region pair protects you from a region failing; a geography tells legal where the data sleeps.==

Not every region has a pair. Newer regions open with three zones and no partner, so their disaster recovery story is "zones plus your own backups to a region you choose". Check the docs before promising anyone a pair.

## Three ways a service can use zones

Zones only help if the service is told to use them. Three postures appear on pricing pages and SKU pickers:

| Posture | What it means | Typical services |
|---|---|---|
| **[Zone-redundant](gl:zone-redundant)** | The service spreads copies across zones automatically; a zone outage is invisible to users | ![Azure SQL](az:azure-sql) Azure SQL Database (zone-redundant option), App Service Premium, zone-redundant storage |
| **Zonal** | You pin an instance to one zone and run copies in other zones yourself | Virtual machines, managed disks, some networking |
| **Regional (non-zonal)** | The service lives somewhere in the region with no zone guarantee | Cheaper tiers, older services, some free plans |

To a stakeholder, "zone-redundant" means: **if one of Microsoft's buildings in the region loses power, the shop stays up and nobody is paged**. It usually costs more - a higher tier, or about one-and-a-half times the storage price - and it does nothing for a failure of the whole region. That is what the pair, and the disaster recovery planning in [lesson 1.7](lesson:1.7), are for.

## Choosing a region

Priya's shop lives in UK South today. Five questions decide whether that stays true and what the India launch needs.

1. **[Latency](gl:latency).** A round trip between London and Mumbai costs over 100 ms before any work is done, and a page needs several. A React app served from the UK feels slow in Bengaluru because every API call pays that tax. Put compute near the users, or at least the static assets.
2. **[Data residency](gl:data-residency) and compliance.** Azure keeps customer data at rest inside the geography you choose. If Indian customer data must stay in India, that is a second deployment in Central India, not a setting on the first.
3. **Price.** The same virtual machine can cost 10-20% more in one region than another. UK South is mid-priced; some US regions are cheaper; some Asian regions are dearer.
4. **Service availability.** New services and hardware land in a handful of large regions first. Confirm every service in your design exists in the region before you commit.
5. **Capacity and quota.** Popular regions run short of particular sizes, and every subscription starts with modest quotas. Request what you need early.

| Region | Geography | Pair | Zones | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| UK South (London) | United Kingdom | UK West (Cardiff) | Yes | Priya's home; the larger of the two |
| UK West | United Kingdom | UK South | No | Smaller catalogue; recovery target |
| West Europe (Netherlands) | Europe | North Europe (Ireland) | Yes | Largest EU catalogue; often capacity-constrained |
| North Europe | Europe | West Europe | Yes | Cheaper than West Europe for many SKUs |
| Central India (Pune) | India | South India (Chennai) | Yes | Natural home for the Mumbai launch |
| South India | India | Central India | No | Smaller; pairing partner |
| East US (Virginia) | United States | West US (California) | Yes | Cheapest and broadest, and the busiest |
| West US | United States | East US | No | Pairing partner; West US 2 and 3 have zones |

This table will be out of date within a year: pairs and zones get added, and services arrive region by region. Treat it as a live document and check the Azure products-by-region page before every architecture decision.

## Sovereign clouds and the edge

Two more pieces of the map sit outside the normal regions.

A **[sovereign cloud](gl:sovereign-cloud)** is a physically and logically separate Azure run for a jurisdiction with its own rules. Azure Government serves US public bodies; Azure China is operated by a local partner, 21Vianet, under Chinese law. Each has its own portal, endpoints and a smaller catalogue. Priya will not need one; a public-sector client might.

At the other end, Azure Front Door and Azure CDN run hundreds of **[points of presence](gl:point-of-presence)**: small caching and TLS-terminating sites in city exchanges worldwide. They hold no customer data at rest, so legal rarely minds them, and they are the cheapest fix for Ana's four seconds: the React bundle is cached in Mumbai while the API and database stay in UK South. ![Front Door](az:front-door)

## In practice

For the UK shop the shape is UK South, zone-redundant App Service and Azure SQL, Front Door in front, and backups geo-replicated to UK West. For India, Priya can offer legal a choice: serve India from the UK behind Front Door (fast for browsing, slower at checkout, all data in the UK), or deploy a second copy in Central India with its own database (fast and residency-compliant, but twice the running cost plus a data-sync problem). Neither is wrong. Residency and latency are decided by geography and region, not by code.

What goes wrong: a team picks the first region in the dropdown and later finds a service missing; a "zone-redundant" claim in a design review turns out to be a Basic tier; and nobody checks that backups actually leave the region until the day they are needed.

## Map it to what you know

| You already know | On Azure |
|---|---|
| Putting your React bundle on a CDN so it loads fast everywhere | Front Door or CDN points of presence caching content near each user |
| SQL Server Always On availability groups across two sites | Zone-redundant Azure SQL inside a region; geo-replication to the paired region |
| A slow API call because the server is on another continent | Latency: the round-trip tax on every request to a far-off region |
| Choosing which hosting data centre your VPS lives in | Choosing a region: the same trade-off of speed, price and law |
| A DR site you fail over to once a year in a drill | The paired region, with maintenance sequencing and priority recovery |

## Guiding your team

> [!TEAM]
> Before anyone creates a resource, ask:
>
> 1. **Which geography must the data stay in, and who signed that off?** Residency is a legal answer, not an engineering preference.
> 2. **Where are the users, and what latency have we measured from there?** Guessing is how you get four-second pages.
> 3. **Is every service in the design available in this region today?** Check the products-by-region page and date the design.
> 4. **Which components are zone-redundant, and what does each cost extra?** Name the tier, not the intent.
> 5. **What happens if the whole region fails?** A pair, a chosen second region, or an accepted outage - but say which.

> [!TIP] Exam link
> AZ-900's "Describe Azure architecture and services" section asks you to define regions, region pairs, sovereign regions and availability zones, and to say why you would pick one over another. The classic trick: zones protect against a data centre failure; region pairs protect against a regional disaster. Keep those apart and the questions are easy.

## Key takeaways

- Data centre, availability zone, region, geography: each layer protects against a bigger failure and answers a bigger question.
- A region is what you choose; a geography is where your data at rest stays; a pair is where Microsoft recovers you first.
- Zone-redundant means a building can fail and users never notice; it costs more and does nothing for a whole-region outage.
- Pick a region on latency, residency, price, service availability and quota - and check the current docs, because all five change.
- Sovereign clouds serve governments under their own rules; edge points of presence make far-away users fast without moving the data.
